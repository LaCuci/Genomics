Our Quality Control pipeline explained

We implemented a sequential quality control pipeline that relies on the concepts we have seen in class. We first generate histograms of missingness and Minor Allele Frequency (MAF), which help us understand the data distribution before establishing strict numerical thresholds that trigger automatic exclusions.

Missingness Control

When we consider how sample and variant filtering affect one another, we must account for the fact that if you remove some individuals whose SNPs had a high NA percentage, then the SNPs NA values are reduced and this will lower the number of SNPs positions that we have to remove due to too many NAs. However, because our histograms reveal that individual missingness is uniformly low across the board, we bypass sample exclusions entirely to preserve our dataset. For our variants, we set an automatic exclusion threshold for SNPs with greater than 2% missingness to eliminate error-prone data.

Allele frequency control and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium

We know that our final goal is to apply statistical tests to understand if some SNP positions are correlated to the phenotype (case/control). It is important to have enough samples carrying the genetic variant, otherwise we lack the statistical power to detect true associations and will only inflate our multiple-testing penalty. This is why we filter by Minor Allele Frequency (MAF).

MAF represents the overall frequency of the less common allele within the dataset. Because every person carries two copies of each SNP, our 494 individuals represent 988 total alleles.

If a specific SNP has a MAF of 1%, only about 10 minor alleles exist (988 * 1/100) in the entire pool. Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) is a model that helps us understand how those alleles pair up into genotypes. It follows the distribution p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1, where p is the frequency of the major allele, and q is the frequency of the minor allele (MAF).

Because rare alleles randomly pair with common ones, these 10 alleles will almost exclusively appear in heterozygous individuals (genotype 1), leaving the homozygous minor group (genotype 2) entirely empty. Testing such rare SNPs provides no actionable statistical power. Consequently, SNPs with a MAF below our threshold are discarded.

We chose a 10% Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) threshold. To justify this choice, we calculate the expected genetic distribution based on our sample size and HWE principles. We previously filtered out SNPs with more than 2% missing values, leaving a minimum of 484 valid individuals (968 alleles).

At a 10% MAF (q=0.1), we retain approximately 97 minor alleles (10% of 968) in the test pool. Under HWE distribution, a 10% MAF ensures that roughly 18% (2pq = 2 * 0.9 * 0.1) of our sample (about 87 individuals) will be heterozygous carriers (genotype 1). This provides a highly robust sub-population for our statistical analysis.

However, HWE also dictates that the frequency of the homozygous minor genotype (genotype 2) will be q^2 = 0.01. In a sample of 484 people, this yields about 5 individuals. While splitting these 5 individuals between cases and controls still yields expected cell counts slightly below Cochran’s rule of 5 (approx. 3 per cell), this 10% threshold drastically reduces the severity of the sparse-data problem compared to a lower threshold.

Therefore, our 10% MAF filter secures our overall statistical power, reduces the multiple-testing penalty, and removes the most mathematically unstable SNPs from the primary analysis. Since we know we are discarding many SNPs, we will evaluate those rare variants separately in Part 5 using Monte Carlo simulations.

Explanation of our Statistic tests

We explain step by step what we did in our statistical analysis

3A

In this section, we test SNP-phenotype associations by applying a 2-degree-of-freedom Pearson Chi-squared test to every variant retained after quality control. We are testing the null hypothesis there is complete independence between disease status and genotype frequencies . We use 2 degrees of freedom because we are comparing a 2x3 contingency table (2 phenotype statuses by 3 possible genotypes) and the degree of freedom is calculated as (2-1)*(3-1)=2

We opted for omitting missing calls entirely to prevent data distortion rather than misinterpreting them as the reference genotype.

To ensure mathematical validity, we record the exact number of individuals contributing to each test and extract the minimum expected cell count. To handle estimation failures, our function verifies data variability, returning NA for monomorphic variants to prevent crashes. Crucially, while our 5% MAF threshold guarantees at least 24 minor genotypes, phenotype imbalances can still push expected cell counts below 20. We calculate and flag these low expected counts to assess the approximation’s reliability, ensuring no warnings are silently discarded.

3B

In this section, we provide a worked example to transparently illustrate our statistical methodology on a single variant. Rather than arbitrarily selecting a variant, we dynamically isolate the SNP with the strongest statistical signal (the lowest raw p-value) from our full analysis. We construct and display the observed 2x3 contingency table to detail the exact case and control counts across all three genotype categories. Next, we compute and print the expected counts under the null hypothesis (no association). This explicitly demonstrates how the raw count data enters the Chi-squared model and allows us to manually verify the assumption that expected cell counts remain adequate for the approximation. Finally, we extract the test statistic, degrees of freedom, and p-value. To ensure rigorous and unbiased evaluation, we implement an automated interpretation block. This dynamically compares the variant’s p-value against both the nominal threshold (p < 0.05) and our conservative Bonferroni threshold, explicitly stating whether we fail to reject the null hypothesis while carefully distinguishing between nominal and genome-wide significance.

3C

Because we are evaluating thousands of variants simultaneously across our genomic family of hypotheses, we control the Family-Wise Error Rate (FWER) by applying a Bonferroni correction, yielding an adjusted p-value.

We plot the −log 10(p-values) of all tested variants against their genomic positions, incorporating visual reference lines for both the nominal significance threshold (p=0.05) and the conservative Bonferroni threshold.

Given the massive number of independent tests (m≈24,350) and our restricted sample size, we anticipate that the stringent Bonferroni threshold will likely not be met by any variant.

Therefore, we complement the analysis with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) evaluation using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Because FDR controls the proportion of false discoveries rather than enforcing an absolute zero-tolerance for false positives, it serves as a more statistically powerful tool to check for less strong signals, ensuring that the results are checked thoroughly before making a conclusion.

# A personalized function is created to test association using the Chi-squared test with 2x3 contingency table

test_snp_chisq <- function(snp_vector) {
  # First of all it removes missing values
  valid_idx <- !is.na(snp_vector) & !is.na(phenotype)
  s <- snp_vector[valid_idx]
  p <- phenotype[valid_idx]
  n <- length(s)
  
  # Check for variability in genotypes and phenotype: The following lines check the variability of both variants and individuals. A 3x2 Chi-squared test requires sufficient observations across genotype categories (0, 1, 2) and phenotypic classes (cases/controls). If data are monomorphic or lack variation, the contingency table will    have empty cells or dimensions smaller than 2x2, causing the statistical test to fail or crash the program. Thus, untestable variants safely return NA.
  
  if (length(unique(s)) <= 1 || length(unique(p)) <= 1) {
    return(c(n = n, statistic = NA, df = NA, min_expected = NA, p_value = NA))
  }
  
  # Contingency table (Genotype: 0, 1, 2 | Phenotype: Controls 0, Cases 1)
  tbl <- table(s, p)
  
  # Perform Chi-squared test
  chisq_res <- suppressWarnings(chisq.test(tbl, correct = FALSE))
  
  return(c(n = n, statistic = unname(chisq_res$statistic),
           df = unname(chisq_res$parameter),
           min_expected = min(chisq_res$expected),
           p_value = chisq_res$p.value))
}

# Apply the test to all retained variants
chisq_results <- t(sapply(as.data.frame(genotypes_clean), test_snp_chisq))

Example insight

The association scan identified a promising nominal signal at variant rs2776932 (raw p=8.76×10 ^ −5), indicating a possible trend. However, when evaluated against our Bonferroni threshold (2.05×10 ^ −6 ) which controls the Family-Wise Error Rate across our 24,350 tests, this variant fails to achieve genome-wide significance, resulting in a classification of nominal-only. To account for the conservative nature of Bonferroni, we implemented an exploratory False Discovery Rate (FDR) analysis via the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, but as its very first rank (i=1) uses the exact same baseline entry gate as Bonferroni, even though the FDR threshold relaxes for subsequent ranks, there is not a single variant in our dataset clears this initial baseline entry gate, no variant can clear any other relaxes treshold. So, the algorithm yields zero significant discoveries (q≤0.05).

Conclusions

The genome-wide association scan successfully evaluated all retained variants, calculating a strict Bonferroni decision threshold of 2.05e-06 (alpha = 0.05 / 24,350 tests) to control for multiple testing. The resulting Manhattan plot maps the -log10(p-values) against their genomic positions, featuring an orange line for nominal significance (p < 0.05) and a red line for the aggressive Bonferroni threshold; an empirical FDR line was intentionally omitted by the algorithm because no variants achieved an adjusted q-value ≤ 0.05. As visualized in the plot and confirmed by our results table, zero variants passed our chosen multiple-testing criterion. The top-associated SNP (highlighted on the plot) shows only nominal significance, meaning that after correcting for the sheer volume of tests, its signal cannot be statistically distinguished from random chance. Because the Chi-squared test does not yield an effect estimate (like an odds ratio), we provided the raw genotype-by-phenotype counts for the top five variants to illustrate their distributions. Ultimately, we fail to reject the null hypothesis, concluding there is no robust evidence of association on chromosome 10. These results are associative, not causal, and the lack of significant findings is likely explained by the limited statistical power of our restricted sample size, the isolation of a single chromosome, and the inability of a basic Chi-squared test to adjust for underlying clinical covariates or population structure.

Explanation of our robustness assessment

For our sensitivity analysis, we decided to investigate whether our strict 10% Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) threshold prematurely discarded variants that were actually associated with the disease. To assess this, we bypassed the MAF filter, effectively evaluating all variants that passed the initial missingness QC. Testing rare variants is tricky because low allele counts create sparse contingency tables, which can invalidate standard Chi-squared tests and yield unreliable p-values. To handle this, we used a two-step approach: we first screened all rare variants with a standard test while tracking minimum expected cell counts, and then re-tested any nominally significant variants (p < 0.05) using Monte Carlo simulations (with 2,000 permutations) to get reliable empirical p-values. Lastly, we adjusted our Bonferroni threshold to account for the total number of tested variants (m_total), making sure we evaluated any potential rare variant signals under appropriately strict significance criteria.

# 2. Fast asymptotic screening on rare variants
# The function returns the p-value and the smallest expected count,
# so that we can say how many tests are unreliable (sparse tables)
# What is the meaning of the retrieved p-values?
# Normally, tiny p-value suggests that the distribution of that variant (0, 1, 2) is
# drastically different between cases and controls while a high p-value suggests
# no evidence of an association.
# By choosing to not apply a MAF threshold and considering rare variants, as done in this part,
# a small p-values suggest a sort of artifact because low allele counts create 
#   sparse contingency tables that invalidate standard asymptotic tests.
test_rare_asympt <- function(snp_vector) {
  valid_idx <- !is.na(snp_vector) & !is.na(phenotype)
  s <- snp_vector[valid_idx]
  p <- phenotype[valid_idx]
  if (length(unique(s)) <= 1 || length(unique(p)) <= 1) {
    return(c(p_value = NA, min_expected = NA))
  }
  tbl <- table(s, p)
  fit <- suppressWarnings(chisq.test(tbl, correct = FALSE))
  c(p_value = fit$p.value, min_expected = min(fit$expected))
}

m_total <- sum(pass_snp_miss)  # primary variants + rare variants
new_bonf_threshold <- 0.05 / m_total

cat("\n=== Sensitivity Analysis Results ===\n")

# 4. Monte Carlo simulation only for nominally significant rare variants (p < 0.05)
# It is a reliability check of the smallest p-values, not a test that can confirm
# Bonferroni significance (with B = 2000 the smallest possible p-value is 1/2001,
# about 5e-4, larger than the threshold)
nominally_sig_rare <- which(rare_p_vals < 0.05)
cat("Rare variants requiring Monte Carlo verification (p < 0.05):", length(nominally_sig_rare), "\n")

if (length(nominally_sig_rare) > 0) {
  set.seed(20261031)
  mc_p_vals <- numeric(length(nominally_sig_rare))
  for (j in seq_along(nominally_sig_rare)) {
    snp_col <- genotypes_rare[[nominally_sig_rare[j]]]
    valid_idx <- !is.na(snp_col) & !is.na(phenotype)
    tbl <- table(snp_col[valid_idx], phenotype[valid_idx])
    mc_p_vals[j] <- chisq.test(tbl, simulate.p.value = TRUE, B = 2000)$p.value
  }
  cat("Smallest Monte Carlo p-value among rare variants:", signif(min(mc_p_vals), 3), "\n")

Conclusion about our Sensitivity Analysis

Our sensitivity analysis proves that our 10% MAF filter was the right call.
